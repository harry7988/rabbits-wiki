# 🦷 Dental Health — Why Hay Is Non-Negotiable

<Info title="Why this page exists">

Dental disease is the **number one hidden trigger of GI stasis**: tooth pain stops a rabbit from chewing hay → gut motility slows → stasis. Many owners think a rabbit "suddenly stopped eating" is a gut problem, when the root is in the teeth.

The only effective prevention is **eating grass hay** — which is why this site repeats "unlimited long-stem hay" as the first rule of rabbit keeping: it solves digestion and teeth at once.

</Info>

## Teeth that never stop growing

Rabbit teeth grow continuously, about **2 mm per week**<cite><a href="#ref1">[1]</a></cite>. The full dentition is 28 teeth, including the small "peg teeth" behind the upper incisors — a key trait distinguishing rabbits from rodents<cite><a href="#ref2">[2]</a></cite>.

All teeth are elodont (open-rooted): like rodent incisors, they must be worn down at the same rate they grow.

## Only hay grinds teeth — pellets cannot

Chewing hay produces the **side-to-side grinding motion** that wears molars down. This motion requires **long, coarse fiber** to generate friction.

Pellets and soft vegetables are crushed vertically and swallowed — no lateral grinding, no wear<cite><a href="#ref3">[3]</a></cite>.

<Warning title="Pellets are nutritionally complete — and dentally useless">

A pellet can match hay's vitamins and calories. It can never replace hay's two physical functions: wearing teeth and stimulating gut motility. A pellet-heavy diet leads directly to overgrown molars, sharp spurs, pain, food refusal, and GI stasis.

</Warning>

## The dental disease spiral

```
Not enough long fiber
   → molars under-worn → overgrowth
   → sharp spurs (points) pierce cheek/tongue
   → painful ulcers
   → avoids chewing hay, selects soft food only
   → even less grinding + gut motility slows
   → 🚨 GI STASIS
```

## Common dental diseases

- **Malocclusion** — teeth do not meet properly; acquired (diet-based, most common) or congenital (common in dwarf breeds)
- **Tooth-root abscess** — jaw swelling with thick, caseous pus; extremely hard to treat, requires surgery + long antibiotics. **Any jaw/facial swelling → vet immediately**<cite><a href="#ref2">[2]</a></cite>
- **Nasolacrimal duct blockage** — upper molar roots press the tear duct: recurring weepy eyes are usually **dental in origin**. Eye drops treat the symptom, not the cause.

## High-risk breeds

Compact-faced (brachycephalic) breeds are most affected: **Netherland Dwarf, Holland Lop, Lionhead, Dutch, Dwarf Hotot**. They need more frequent dental checks<cite><a href="#ref3">[3]</a></cite>.

## Home check (weekly)

- Flip the upper lip: 4 upper incisors should be **even, aligned, white-to-ivory**
- Feel along the jaw and cheeks: **no lumps or bumps** (abscess?)
- Watch eating: no **food dropping (quidding)**, no selective refusal of hay
- No **drooling** (wet/stained chin), no loud tooth **grinding** (pain signal — distinct from soft contented purring)

Molars — where most disease lives — **cannot be checked at home**. Annual vet exams still required (twice yearly for dwarf breeds).

<VetCheck>

- Any incisor abnormality (curved, overgrown, discolored)
- Jaw/facial swelling or palpable lump
- Drooling + refusing food
- Food dropping from the mouth
- Recurring weepy eyes
- Stops eating hay but still eats pellets (early molar-spike sign — not pickiness)

</VetCheck>

## Prevention = hay

| Do | Why |
|---|---|
| **Unlimited long-stem hay** (1st/2nd cut, stemmy) | The only irreplaceable grinding mechanism |
| **Limit pellets** | A full rabbit skips hay |
| Annual vet dental checks | Catches acquired disease early |
| ❌ Chew sticks/stones as substitutes | Vertical biting, no lateral grinding — enrichment at best |

**Bottom line**: unlimited long-stem hay simultaneously protects the gut and the teeth. There is no alternative and no exception.
